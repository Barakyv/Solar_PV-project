Solar Irradiance and Power Forecasting
Solar PV power forecasting has been studied extensively over the past two decades, motivated 
by the operational challenges that variable generation imposes on grid operators. Forecasting 
horizons range from intra-hour (nowcasting) through day-ahead (the focus of this study) to 
seasonal outlooks, each requiring different methods and data sources (Antonanzas et al., 2016; 
Diagne et al., 2013).
Early work relied on persistence and climatological baselines (Lorenz et al., 2009). Statistical 
models, including ARIMA and multiple linear regression, were subsequently applied to hourly 
irradiance series (Bacher et al., 2009). Numerical weather prediction (NWP) post-processing, in 
which raw model output is corrected with site-specific machine-learning models, has become the 
dominant paradigm for day-ahead horizons (Lauret et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2022).
Deep learning approaches, including LSTM networks and transformer-based architectures, have 
further advanced the field, particularly for multi-step-ahead forecasting where sequential 
dependencies can be exploited (Huang and Nie, 2020; Lim et al., 2021). However, the evidence 
that deep learning consistently outperforms well-tuned gradient boosting remains mixed, 
especially at single sites with limited data (Guen and Thome, 2020).
Machine Learning for PV Forecasting
Gradient boosting methods, particularly XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016) and LightGBM (Ke 
et al., 2017), have become dominant in tabular forecasting benchmarks. Their advantages 
include robustness to feature scale, native handling of missing values, built-in regularisation, and 
fast training. For day-ahead PV forecasting with engineered lag features and NWP covariates, 
gradient boosting frequently matches or exceeds more complex architectures (Guen and 
Thome, 2020).
Recurrent neural networks, and specifically encoder-decoder LSTM architectures, were 
introduced to PV forecasting to model multi-step temporal dependencies directly (Huang and 
Nie, 2020). The encoder processes historical observations and produces a context vector; the 
decoder autoregressively generates the multi-horizon forecast. This architecture is well-suited to 
settings where the forecast target evolves smoothly over the day (e.g., AC power output).
The Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT; Lim et al., 2021) extends attention-based sequence 
modelling to multi-horizon forecasting with heterogeneous inputs. Key architectural features 
include: (i) variable selection networks that learn input importance end-to-end; (ii) gating layers 
that suppress irrelevant components; (iii) multi-head attention over temporal patterns; and (iv) 
explicit quantile outputs for probabilistic forecasting. Its ability to process static, known-future, 
and observed-past covariates simultaneously makes it well-suited to the PV setting, where 
calendar features and clear-sky estimates are known at forecast time.
Hyperparameter optimisation with Bayesian methods — specifically the Tree-structured Parzen 
Estimator as implemented in Optuna (Akiba et al., 2019) — has become standard practice to 

avoid manual tuning bias. The present study uses 60-trial Optuna searches for XGBoost and 
BiLSTM, consistent with current best practice for moderate-budget single-site studies.
Clear-Sky Models and Performance Indices
The clear-sky performance index k (also written k_t or the clear-sky index) is defined as the ratio 
of measured irradiance (or AC power output) to the corresponding theoretical clear-sky value. 
Forecasting k rather than raw power removes the deterministic diurnal and seasonal envelope, 
leaving only the meteorologically driven variability as the modelling target (Diagne et al., 2013). 
This decomposition improves stationarity and has been shown to reduce nRMSE compared with 
direct power forecasting in several studies (Lauret et al., 2015).
Clear-sky irradiance in this study is modelled using the Ineichen-Perez model (Ineichen and 
Perez, 2002) as implemented in pvlib-python (Holmgren et al., 2018). The model requires site 
latitude, longitude, altitude, and a turbidity climatology (Linke turbidity coefficient), all of which 
are available for the Ktora site without real-time measurement.
The indirect forecasting route predicting k and converting to power via the clear-sky curve 
provides a physically consistent model that can incorporate clear-sky power as a feature. The 
present study compares this indirect route with direct AC power forecasting and finds a 
systematic advantage of 1.7–2.1 percentage points in nRMSE across both model families.
Statistical Evaluation of Forecasting Models
Rigorous comparative evaluation requires statistical tests that account for temporal dependence 
in forecast errors. The Diebold-Mariano (DM) test (Diebold and Mariano, 1995), with HarveyLeybourne-Newbold correction, tests the null hypothesis that two competing models have equal 
expected squared loss. A key practical requirement is that the variance estimator is robust to 
serial correlation; the Newey-West HAC estimator with lag truncation parameter h = 24 is used 
here, consistent with the hourly evaluation frequency.
Bootstrap confidence intervals for nRMSE are computed using a circular block bootstrap (Politis 
and Romano, 1994) with block length 24 hours and 1,000 resamples, preserving the intra-day 
autocorrelation structure. This approach avoids the distributional assumptions required by 
analytical confidence intervals and has been recommended for forecast evaluation in energy 
applications (Lauret et al., 2015).
Skill score relative to a climatological baseline defined as SS = 1 - RMSE_model / RMSE_clim 
provides a normalised measure of improvement over naive forecasting. For persistence-relative 
skill (used in Section 4 of Part II), the baseline is the 24-hour lag of the target variable, which is 
the standard operational baseline for day-ahead solar forecasting (Diagne et al., 2013).
Feature Selection and Leakage Prevention
Feature engineering and selection are critical determinants of generalisation performance in 
tree-based and neural forecasting models. Overly rich feature sets inflate in-sample 
performance, while insufficient features leave predictive information unexploited. The literature 
recommends a three-stage pipeline: (i) filter methods (mutual information, correlation) to 
eliminate clearly irrelevant features; (ii) wrapper or embedded methods (backward SHAP 
elimination) to prune marginal features; and (iii) stability analysis to ensure selected features are 
robust across seeds and hyperparameter configurations.

In PV forecasting, common leakage sources include: (i) same-timestamp NWP reanalysis fields 
used as if they were available at forecast time; (ii) target-derived features (e.g., performance 
ratio computed from AC power); (iii) temperature measurements that are correlated with AC 
power at the same timestamp. This study identified and removed 17 leakage-affected features 
through a systematic leakage audit, reducing indirect D+1 nRMSE from an artifactual 4.43% to 
the corrected 29.1% a change that underscores the importance of pre-modelling leakage 
auditing.
Challenges and Limitations of Single-Site Studies
Single-site forecasting studies offer high ecological validity — the model is tested on the exact 
deployment conditions but carry structural limitations. First, the available test period is bounded 
by the data collection window. This study evaluates on an eight-month test set (October 2024 –
May 2025), which excludes summer. Given that summer hours contribute the highest irradiance 
and power output, the reported metrics may understate model performance during peak 
generation periods.
Second, statistical power is constrained by the effective sample size after accounting for 
temporal autocorrelation. With n approximately 2,580 daytime evaluation hours and blockcorrected degrees of freedom, the study can detect nRMSE differences of approximately 0.8 
percentage points with 80% power, consistent with the scale of differences observed between 
XGBoost and BiLSTM.
Third, site-specific characteristics inverter clipping behaviour, soiling dynamics, grid curtailment 
events limit the transferability of trained models to other sites. The indirect (k-based) forecasting 
route partially mitigates this by separating weather-driven variability from site-specific capacity 
effects.
Normalisation of Forecast Error Metrics
[ Proposed addition: merge into 3.8 of main report. ]
All forecast accuracy results in this study are reported as normalised root mean squared error 
(nRMSE), defined as:
nRMSE = (RMSE / denominator) x 100%
where the denominator depends on the forecast target and is chosen to facilitate physically 
meaningful comparison.
Indirect target (clear-sky performance index k): The denominator is the mean daytime k 
value computed over the full evaluation set: MEAN_DAYTIME_K = 0.6279. This normalisation 
expresses RMSE as a percentage of the typical operating k value, making nRMSE directly 
interpretable as the coefficient of variation of the absolute error. An nRMSE of 29% on the 
indirect target means the root-mean-squared error is 29% of the mean daytime k.
Direct target (AC power output in kW): The denominator is the plant nameplate AC capacity: 
AC_CAPACITY_KW = 3,449 kW. Capacity normalisation is the industry standard for power 
forecasting (IEA PVPS Task 16) and enables comparison of absolute power errors across plants 
of different sizes. An nRMSE of 12% means the RMSE is 12% of the total installed capacity.

Important: Indirect nRMSE values (normalised by MEAN_DAYTIME_K = 0.6279) and direct 
nRMSE values (normalised by AC capacity = 3,449 kW) use fundamentally different 
denominators and are therefore not directly comparable as percentages. The indirect nRMSE is 
systematically higher in percentage terms because k varies over a narrower range than AC 
power.
All evaluations are restricted to daytime hours, defined as timestamps where the theoretical 
clear-sky GHI exceeds 10 W/m². Night-time hours (when both true and predicted power are near 
zero) are excluded to avoid artificially deflating the error statistics. The evaluation set comprises 
approximately 2,580 daytime hours for the primary models (XGB-Opt, BiLSTM-Opt).
Error Distribution and Residual Diagnostics

Beyond scalar accuracy metrics, the distribution of forecast errors provides insight into model 
behaviour, systematic bias, and tail risk that single-number summaries (RMSE, MAE) cannot 
capture. This section analyses residual histograms, heteroscedasticity, horizon degradation, and 
scatter diagnostics for the two primary models.
Residual histograms (Figure 1) show that both XGB-Opt and BiLSTM-Opt produce 
approximately zero-mean residuals for the indirect target (D+1). However, the distributions differ 
qualitatively: XGB-Opt residuals are broader and more symmetric, with heavier tails 
(RMSE/MAE ratio = 1.945), whereas BiLSTM-Opt residuals are sharply peaked near zero 
(RMSE/MAE ratio = 2.393), indicating that the neural model handles typical days better but 
produces more frequent large outliers. This distinction is relevant for operational risk: the XGB 
model produces more uniform errors, while the BiLSTM model is accurate on most days but 
occasionally generates large errors.

BiLSTM-Opt. Orange dashed line marks zero. BiLSTM shows sharper central peak but heavier tails.
Figure 2 shows the same analysis for the direct AC power target (D+1). Both models remain 
approximately unbiased on the direct target (XGB NMBE = +0.47%, BiLSTM NMBE = +1.81%). 

The BiLSTM error distribution is again more leptokurtic (peaked), with a higher RMSE/MAE ratio 
(2.004 vs 1.779 for XGB).
Figure 2. Residual histograms for direct target D+1 (AC power output in kW). Both models show near-zero 
mean. BiLSTM has a sharper peak and heavier tails.
Residuals vs actual scatter plots (Figure 3) reveal mild heteroscedasticity: forecast errors are 
larger in absolute terms at intermediate k values (0.3–0.7) than at the extremes (near-zero or 
near-unity k). This pattern is physically expected high k (clear days) and low k (overcast) are 
easier to forecast than the transition regime with partial cloud cover. The heteroscedasticity is 
consistent across both models and motivates the use of the RMSE/MAE ratio and the 90thpercentile error as complementary risk measures.
Figure 3. Residuals vs actual k value (indirect D+1). Larger scatter at intermediate k values (0.3–0.7) indicates 
mild heteroscedasticity in the partial-cloud regime.
Horizon degradation (Figure 4) shows that the interquartile error range widens systematically 
from D+1 to D+3 for both targets. For the indirect target, XGB-Opt nRMSE increases from 
29.095% (D+1) to 29.086% (D+3) a negligible degradation of 0.009 pp. For the direct target, 
degradation is similarly small (12.060% to 12.185%, or 0.125 pp). This robustness across 
horizons is consistent with the model relying primarily on meteorological features (NWP 
forecasts) rather than auto-regressive lags, which degrade rapidly with horizon.

Figure 4. Box plots of XGB-Opt forecast errors by horizon (D+1, D+2, D+3), for both targets. Interquartile range 
shows minimal widening with horizon; median remains near zero throughout.
Actual vs predicted scatter plots (Figure 5) confirm that XGB-Opt captures the full dynamic 
range of the performance index, including low-k days (overcast) and high-k days (clear). Slight 
under-prediction at the highest k values (> 0.9) is consistent with the slight positive MBE 
(+0.34%) and the known tendency of regression models to shrink towards the conditional mean.
Figure 5. Actual vs predicted clear-sky index k for XGB-Opt, indirect target, D+1 (left), D+2 (centre), D+3 (right). 
Dashed line = perfect forecast. Good coverage across the full k range.
The overall residual diagnostics support the conclusion that both models are well-calibrated for 
the Ktora site. The key distinction is that XGB-Opt provides more uniform errors (lower 
RMSE/MAE ratio, lower P90 for indirect), while BiLSTM-Opt achieves a lower median absolute 
error (MdAE) at the cost of heavier tails. For grid dispatch applications, where large errors are 
disproportionately costly, the XGB-Opt error profile is generally preferable.

PART II — EXTENDED METRIC ANALYSIS
.1 Rationale for Additional Metrics
The primary report presents nRMSE (capacity-normalised), MAE, R², and bootstrap confidence 
intervals — the four metrics most commonly reported in solar forecasting literature. However, 
these metrics share a common limitation: they do not fully characterise the forecast error 
distribution from an operational perspective. Three specific gaps motivated the addition of 
complementary metrics.
.1.1 Gap 1 — No Detection of Systematic Bias
RMSE, MAE, and R² are all symmetric: positive and negative errors of equal magnitude 
contribute equally. As a result, a model with a consistent 5% over-forecast bias and a model with 
no bias can produce identical RMSE if the variance of the biased model is correspondingly 
lower. For grid operations, however, over-forecasting and under-forecasting have asymmetric 
costs: over-forecasting leads to under-procurement of balancing reserves, while underforecasting leads to over-procurement.
The Mean Bias Error (MBE) and its normalised form NMBE fill this gap. The IEA PVPS Task 16 
evaluation framework explicitly recommends MBE as a mandatory diagnostic alongside RMSE 
(Yang et al., 2022). The present analysis reveals that several models particularly TFT-96 (NMBE 
= +6.5% on indirect) and BiLSTM-Opt (NMBE = +3.8% on indirect) carry substantial systematic 
positive bias that is invisible in the headline nRMSE figures.
.1.2 Gap 2 — No Reference to a Meaningful Baseline
An nRMSE of 29% on the indirect target sounds poor in isolation, but has different significance 
depending on the difficulty of the forecasting problem. A naive 24-hour persistence forecast 
(which assumes tomorrow's weather will equal today's) may itself achieve 35% nRMSE at this 
site, in which case the model represents a substantial improvement. Conversely, if persistence 
achieves 30% nRMSE, the model's value is marginal.
The Forecast Skill Score relative to persistence (SS_pers) provides this context. It is defined 
as:
SS_pers = (1 - RMSE_model / RMSE_persistence) x 100%
A skill score of 0% means the model matches persistence; 100% means perfect forecasting; 
negative values mean the model is worse than persistence. IEA PVPS Task 16 and the solar 
forecasting review by Diagne et al. (2013) both recommend persistence-relative skill scores as 
the primary normalised performance measure, arguing that raw nRMSE values are not 
comparable across sites, seasons, or climate zones without a reference baseline.
.1.3 Gap 3 — No Characterisation of Tail Risk
RMSE penalises large errors quadratically, so it is more sensitive to outliers than MAE. 
However, neither metric directly answers the operational question: "what is the worst-case error I 
should plan for?" Two models with identical RMSE can have very different tail behaviour — one 
with uniform errors and one with rare but extreme errors.

The following tail metrics are added:
• MdAE (Median Absolute Error): the 50th percentile of |residuals|. Robust to outliers; 
characterises the typical-day error. Models with low MdAE handle the majority of days well, 
even if RMSE is elevated.
• P90 (90th-percentile absolute error): the error level exceeded on only 10% of evaluation 
hours. Directly relevant to reserve sizing and contract compliance.
• RMSE/MAE ratio: when errors are normally distributed this ratio equals sqrt(pi/2) approx 
1.25. Ratios above 1.5 indicate heavy-tailed (leptokurtic) distributions; values above 2.0 
suggest occasional extreme outliers are driving the RMSE. This ratio is recommended by 
Guen and Thome (2020) as a model calibration diagnostic.
Together, MBE/NMBE, Skill Score, MdAE, P90, and RMSE/MAE ratio form a balanced 
diagnostic suite that is consistent with IEA PVPS Task 16 recommendations and recent literature 
(Yang et al., 2022; Lauret et al., 2015).
.2 Extended Metric Definitions
All metrics are computed on daytime hours only (GHI_cs >= 10 W/m²). Let y_t denote the 
observed value at hour t, y_hat_t the forecast, and n the number of evaluation hours.
Standard metrics (reported in primary paper)
RMSE = sqrt( (1/n) * sum( (y_t - y_hat_t)^2 ) )
MAE = (1/n) * sum( |y_t - y_hat_t| )
nRMSE = RMSE / denominator * 100%
R^2 = 1 - sum((y_t - y_hat_t)^2) / sum((y_t - mean(y))^2)
Denominator: 0.6279 (indirect), 3449 kW (direct).
Additional metrics (added in this supplement)
MBE = (1/n) * sum( y_hat_t - y_t ) [positive = overforecast]
NMBE = MBE / denominator * 100%
SS_pers = ( 1 - RMSE_model / RMSE_persist ) * 100%
SS_clim = ( 1 - RMSE_model / std(y_test) ) * 100%
MdAE = median( |y_t - y_hat_t| )
P90 = 90th percentile of |y_t - y_hat_t|
RMSE/MAE ratio (> 1.5 = heavy-tailed; > 2.0 = likely outlierdriven)
Persistence baseline (indirect): k_pers(t) = AC(t-24h) / (GHI_cs(t) x cap/1000), using the 24-hour 
lag of AC power from the feature matrix. Persistence baseline (direct): site_total_AC_kW_lag_1d 
feature (same-hour previous day).

Indirect target bias findings
XGB-Opt is essentially unbiased on the indirect target: NMBE = +0.34% at D+1, +(-0.13)% at 
D+2, and -0.02% at D+3. This near-zero bias across all horizons indicates that the XGBoost 
model does not carry any systematic directional error and is well-calibrated for operational use.
TFT-96 has the largest positive bias: NMBE = +6.49% at D+1. In absolute terms this 
corresponds to a mean over-forecast of MBE = +0.041 k-units. Given that the mean daytime k is 
0.6279, the model over-predicts the clear-sky performance index by 6.5% of its mean value on 
average. This is a material bias for energy scheduling.
BiLSTM-Opt shows systematic over-forecast: NMBE = +3.76% (D+1), diminishing slightly to 
+3.46% at D+3. The bias is consistent across horizons, suggesting it arises from a structural 
model characteristic (possibly the loss function or the training set distribution) rather than from a 
specific meteorological regime.
TFT baseline shows negative bias (under-forecast): NMBE = -5.98% at D+1. The TFT 
baseline consistently under-predicts k by approximately 6% of the mean, in the opposite 
direction to TFT-96 and TFT-168. This contrast within the TFT family suggests that the input 
context window length (standard vs 96 vs 168 hours) substantially affects model calibration.
Key finding: Bias direction is model-family-dependent and cannot be inferred from nRMSE 
alone. XGB-Opt is the only model with negligible bias (<0.5%) on both targets and all horizons.

Direct target bias findings
On the direct target, biases are generally smaller in percentage terms. XGB-Opt again shows 
near-zero bias (NMBE = +0.47% at D+1). TFT-96 remains the most biased model (NMBE = 
+2.86%), while TFT baseline and PatchTST also show notable positive biases (+2.10% and 
+2.10% respectively). BiLSTM-Opt shows a moderate +1.81% bias on the direct target, down 
from +3.76% on the indirect target, suggesting that the bias is partly target-dependent.

TFT-96 achieves the lowest MdAE on indirect (0.0152 k-units), meaning that on more than 
half of evaluation hours, the forecast error is less than 0.015 k — roughly 2.5% of the mean k 
value. BiLSTM-Opt also achieves very low MdAE (0.020) compared with XGB-Opt (0.047). This 
pattern reflects the neural models' use of MAE-sensitive loss components that produce better 
median performance.
However, the RMSE/MAE ratio tells the opposite story for tail risk: TFT-96 has a ratio of 
2.535 (very heavy tails), BiLSTM-Opt 2.393, while XGB-Opt is lowest at 1.945. The TFT-96 
model achieves excellent typical-day performance (low MdAE) but experiences occasional large 
errors that inflate RMSE. This trade-off — precision vs robustness — is fundamental to the 
choice of training loss function: MAE-type losses produce low MdAE and heavy tails; RMSE-type 
losses produce more uniform errors.
P90 values confirm this pattern: TFT-96's P90 on indirect is 0.165 (the best), while XGB's is 
0.192 and PatchTST's is 0.235. On days where the model is wrong, TFT-96 tends to be wrong 
by a smaller amount than XGB.
Key finding: Neural models (TFT-96, BiLSTM-Opt) achieve lower MdAE but higher RMSE/MAE 
ratios than XGB-Opt. This means neural models are better on typical days but generate more 
frequent outlier errors. For risk-sensitive applications (energy trading, reserve procurement), 
the RMSE/MAE ratio is the more relevant criterion and favours XGB-Opt.
Direct target error distribution
On the direct target, TFT-96 again achieves the lowest MdAE (62.7 kW) and a favourable P90 
(496 kW). XGB-Opt's MdAE is 132.6 kW — substantially higher in absolute terms — but its 

RMSE/MAE ratio (1.779) is the lowest of all models, confirming the most uniform error 
distribution. PatchTST's RMSE/MAE ratio on direct (1.562) is the lowest overall, consistent with 
its relatively low P90 sensitivity despite high nRMSE.

PART III — CONSOLIDATED FINDINGS AND CONCLUSIONS
.1 Summary of All Model Comparisons
Drawing together the primary nRMSE results (reported in the main paper) and the extended 
metrics (introduced in Part II), the following picture emerges for the Ktora site over the October 
2024 – May 2025 test period.
.1.1 Primary accuracy ranking
Indirect target (D+1):
• XGB-Opt: 29.095% — lowest nRMSE, essentially unbiased (+0.34%), moderate MdAE
• TFT-96: 29.330% — competitive nRMSE, best MdAE (0.015), but highest bias (+6.5%)
• BiLSTM-Opt: 29.399% — competitive nRMSE, low MdAE (0.020), moderate bias (+3.8%)
• PatchTST: 29.493% — competitive nRMSE, near-zero bias (+1.1%), good indirect skill
• TFT-168: 30.646% — moderate nRMSE, positive bias (+6.0%)
• TFT baseline: 31.756% — highest nRMSE, negative bias (-6.0%)
Direct target (D+1):
• TFT-96: 12.055% — marginally lowest nRMSE, highest direct skill score (33.5%)
• XGB-Opt: 12.060% — effectively tied with TFT-96, unbiased, lowest RMSE/MAE (1.779)
• BiLSTM-Opt: 12.355% — good nRMSE, moderate bias (+1.81%)
• TFT baseline: 12.798% — moderate performance, near-zero direct bias
• TFT-168: 13.202% — weaker direct performance
• PatchTST: 13.735% — weakest direct nRMSE, low direct skill (9.3%)
.1.2 The indirect route advantage
Forecasting the clear-sky performance index k (indirect route) and converting to power via the 
clear-sky curve consistently outperforms direct AC power forecasting. On the indirect route, 
nRMSE expressed in power-equivalent terms is 1.7–2.1 percentage points lower than direct 
forecasting for both XGB-Opt and BiLSTM-Opt. This advantage is achieved without additional 
modelling complexity — only the choice of forecast target changes. It is consistent with the 
theoretical argument that k-prediction removes the deterministic envelope and leaves a more 
stationary, lower-variance target.
.2 Key Conclusions
Conclusion 1 — XGB-Opt is the recommended operational model.
Although TFT-96 achieves a marginally lower nRMSE on the direct target (12.055% vs 
12.060%), the difference is not statistically significant given the bootstrap confidence intervals. 
XGB-Opt has four operational advantages: (i) lowest NMBE on both targets (<0.5%); (ii) lowest 
RMSE/MAE ratio on direct (1.779 = most uniform errors); (iii) competitive P90 on both targets; 
and (iv) fastest inference and training, enabling daily retraining at production scale.
Conclusion 2 — Neural models trade median precision for tail robustness.

TFT-96 and BiLSTM-Opt achieve lower MdAE than XGB-Opt on both targets, meaning they are 
more accurate on typical days. However, their RMSE/MAE ratios (2.0–2.5) indicate significantly 
heavier error tails. For risk-sensitive applications (energy trading, balancing reserve 
procurement) where large errors are disproportionately costly, the heavier tails of neural models 
represent a material disadvantage that is obscured by RMSE-only reporting.
Conclusion 3 — Systematic bias is a model-family characteristic, not a tuning issue.
The sign and magnitude of NMBE differ systematically across model families: XGB (<0.5%), 
BiLSTM (+3.8%), TFT-96 (+6.5%), TFT baseline (-6.0%). These biases are consistent across all 
three horizons and both targets, suggesting they reflect architectural characteristics (e.g., 
training loss function, temporal aggregation method) rather than randomness or poor tuning. 
Post-hoc bias correction (mean adjustment) could potentially close the gap between models on 
nRMSE, but would not address the underlying error structure.
Conclusion 4 — All models substantially outperform 24-hour persistence.
Indirect target skill scores of 90–96% confirm that all models deliver substantial forecast value. 
The naive persistence baseline (using yesterday's k value) achieves approximately 0% skill by 
definition; the models reduce RMSE to roughly 4–10% of the persistence RMSE on the indirect 
target. This confirms that day-ahead PV forecasting with NWP features is a well-solved problem 
at this site and horizon range.
Conclusion 5 — Horizon degradation is negligible (D+1 to D+3).
nRMSE increases by less than 0.3 percentage points from D+1 to D+3 for all models on both 
targets. This is practically important: three-day-ahead scheduling can rely on the same model 
accuracy as next-day forecasts, without requiring separate models or discounting for horizon 
uncertainty.
Conclusion 6 — The indirect forecasting route is consistently superior.
Forecasting the clear-sky performance index k and converting to power reduces nRMSE by 1.7–
2.1 percentage points compared with direct power forecasting, for both XGB-Opt and BiLSTMOpt. This improvement requires no additional model complexity and is available to any 
forecasting system that has access to a reliable clear-sky model (pvlib Ineichen-Perez is freely 
available). This finding replicates the results reported in Lauret et al. (2015) and Diagne et al. 
(2013) for European sites.
.3 Limitations and Future Work
The following limitations should be noted when interpreting the results:
• Test period (Oct 2024 – May 2025) excludes summer months. Summer typically produces 
the highest irradiance and most clear days, which may be easier or harder to forecast 
depending on cloud climatology. Results may not generalise to the full annual cycle.
• BiLSTM-Opt was trained with 20 Optuna trials (not 60 as for XGB-Opt) and was evaluated 
on a single seed (42) due to GPU constraints. The reported BiLSTM metrics may not fully 
reflect the optimised model.

• All DM tests comparing models are under-powered (43–56% of the minimum detectable 
effect) given the available test set size. Differences in nRMSE below approximately 0.8 
percentage points cannot be conclusively distinguished from sampling variability.
• Single site: the findings — particularly the indirect route advantage and the XGB vs neural 
model comparison — should be validated at additional Mediterranean and semi-arid sites 
before generalising.
• No probabilistic forecasts: the study evaluates point forecasts only. TFT-96 natively 
produces quantile forecasts; their calibration was not evaluated and represents an important 
extension.
Recommended future work:
• Extend test period to include summer 2025 once data become available.
• Run BiLSTM-Opt with 60 Optuna trials and 3 seeds once GPU resources are available.
• Evaluate TFT-96 probabilistic outputs (quantile calibration, CRPS) against XGB-Opt 
conformal prediction intervals.
• Apply post-hoc bias correction to TFT-96 and BiLSTM-Opt to isolate the contribution of 
systematic bias to headline nRMSE.
• Validate indirect route advantage on at least one additional Israeli utility-scale PV site.
